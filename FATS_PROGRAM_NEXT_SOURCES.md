Titles are ones I am confident exist. Where I am not sure a book covers the point, I say so. The program's rule stands: anything I add from a web source stays tiered SECONDARY or UNVERIFIED until you have the book.

## 2. Regional holes in the overviews

### Near East, 1500–1900 (no entry in three folios except one Syrian manual of about 1892)
- **GET** Beshara Doumani, *Rediscovering Palestine: Merchants and Peasants in Jabal Nablus, 1700–1900* (University of California Press, 1995). Nablus soap and olive oil are the heart of its economy. This would give `fats-1750-1850` and `fats-1850-1900` a real Ottoman entry.
- **GET (maybe)** Bruce Masters, *The Origins of Western Economic Dominance in the Middle East: Mercantilism and the Islamic Economy in Aleppo, 1600–1750* (1988). I am not sure how much it says on soap and oil; check the index for "soap" and "olive" before spending time.
- **LIVE WITHOUT** a Safavid entry; I know no standard book that treats fats there.

### Africa, 600–1750
- **GET** Richard Pankhurst, *An Economic History of Ethiopia, 1800–1935* (1968): butter, oilseeds and noug exports. It would also seed the Ethiopia gap in `fats-600-1000` (only as later evidence, so I would date it honestly).
- **GET** Jack Goody, *Cooking, Cuisine and Class* (1982): compares West African and Eurasian fats (shea, palm oil, butter). Check the index for "shea" and "oil". This is also the best lead for shea after Kirikongo and for Mali, both flagged as gaps in `fats-1000-1500`.
- **ALT** Dahl & Hjort, *Having Herds* (1976), cited in Kiple's camel chapter, for pastoral milk fat in East Africa; the Kiple entry already carries the main figures.
- **LIVE WITHOUT, checked 2026-10-25**: the Swahili coast. I checked the Goitein volumes we already hold (strong on Indian Ocean trade) specifically for this — they do mention East Africa (the port of ʿAydhāb, Abyssinian slaves, Ethiopic church history), but nothing about fats or oils; it is slave-trade and transit-customs material. No candidate source for Swahili-coast coconut, sesame or ghee trade exists yet.
- **LIVE WITHOUT**: Nubia's ancient/medieval fat economy. No title at all, not even an unread candidate.

### Americas, before 1500 and colonial
- **DONE 2026-10-25.** You added four books to the Food History folder: *Maritime Communities of the Ancient Andes* (Prieto & Sandweiss, eds., 2019), Goepfert & Faugère's *Atlas de l'Amérique précolombienne* (2022), Sophie Coe's *America's First Cuisines* (1994), and the *Oxford Handbook of the Archaeology of Diet* (Lee-Thorp & Katzenberg, eds., 2024). All four were mined and yielded four new entries: `fats-8000bc-1200bc::ec29` (marrow and grease extraction rising with camelid hunting on the Lake Junín puna, c.4000–2000 BC, from the Oxford Handbook's Peru/Ecuador chapter), `fats-1000-1500::ec15` (the Chimú town of Cerro la Virgen, whose middens say most meat came from camelids, not fish — from *Maritime Communities*, citing Pozorski 1979), `fats-8000bc-1200bc::te5` (Coe's own argument that without a press or quern the New World could only boil its oil seeds, and rarely ate what it got), and `fats-1500-1750::ec19` (a Spanish colonist in Nicaragua served fish and eggs cooked in cacao fat, mistaking it for lard — Coe quoting Oviedo). Pushed with the overview region updates.
- **Mesoamerica was a complete blank across the whole 130,000-year program before this pass.** Coe's book closed part of that (cacao fat, the technology argument, chia oil used for paint/varnish/waterproofing — see `fats-8000bc-1200bc::te5`'s note for Coe's own caution about chia's pre-Columbian date). The Classic Maya (300 BC–1000 AD) specifically are still untouched — Coe's book is about the whole of pre-Columbian Mesoamerica and the Andes, not an isotope/lipid study of any one Maya site.
- The Goepfert atlas (in French) pointed to a mass camelid sacrifice at Huanchaquito-Las Llamas (c. AD 1450, Chimú, read directly via the open-access PLOS ONE paper): 200 camelids and 140 children, selected by age and coat colour, in a likely response to an El Niño flood event. **Not added as a fats entry** — the paper is explicit that camelids were chosen for age and colour, not for their fat, meat or any economic role, so it would be forcing a fit. A paper the article cites but we have not fetched, Dufour et al. 2018 "Provisioning a Mass Killing Sacrifice During the Chimú Period," might give the camelid-economy angle if you want it chased.
- The Oxford Handbook's Maya chapter (ch. 20, "An Isotopic Anthropology of Ancient Maya Diets") and its Peru/Ecuador chapter (ch. 21) are both isotope-based diet studies (C/N ratios, trophic levels) — they say almost nothing about fat specifically (one "lipid" mention in the Maya chapter, in a methods aside). **LIVE WITHOUT** this handbook for the Maya/Moche-era fat gap specifically; it answers "how much protein, from what," not "how much fat."
- **GET (Maya, Classic period, 300 BC–1000 AD), still open**: Christine White's isotope/dietary papers on the Maya (with Fred Longstaffe), or the Florida Museum's Maya lipid-residue project (floridamuseum.ufl.edu/envarch/research/maya) — only their project page was read, not their actual papers; a name that surfaced was Lisa Duffy.
- **GET, found but paywalled 2026-10-25**: two real, specific Preclassic Maya zooarchaeology papers from Belize, both potentially useful for the Maya gap AND for the dog/turkey-fat question below: Clutton-Brock, J. and Hammond, N., "Hot dogs: Comestible Canids in Preclassic Maya Culture at Cuello, Belize," *Journal of Archaeological Science* 21(6) (1994), pp.819–826, doi:10.1006/jasc.1994.1079; and White, C.D., Pohl, M.D., Schwarcz, H.P. and Longstaffe, F.J., "Isotopic Evidence for Maya Patterns of Deer and Dog Use at Preclassic Colha," *Journal of Archaeological Science* 28(1) (2001), pp.89–107, doi:10.1006/jasc.1999.0560. Both are on ScienceDirect/Elsevier (403 Forbidden to automated fetch); no open-access copy found via academia.edu, ResearchGate or a general search. These are the two papers most likely to say something concrete about Preclassic Maya dog-feeding and, by extension, dog fat.
- **CHECKED 2026-10-25, does NOT hold up**: "dogs bred/fattened specifically for meat and fat" in Mesoamerica. A 2010 *Archaeology* magazine piece on Olmec dogs at San Lorenzo has only a hedged "perhaps the dogs were being deliberately fattened" — speculation, not a finding — and cites an unnamed isotope study (dogs ate only maize; that shows what they were fed, not that they were fattened for fat). Wikipedia's claim that dogs were "10% of all consumed meat in Teotihuacan" has no traceable citation. Nothing found anywhere mentions dog *fat* specifically, as opposed to dog meat generally. The Xoloitzcuintli/Techichi "bred for royal feasts" story is repeated everywhere online but sourced nowhere I could verify; one osteological study of an Aztec-period dog burial found ordinary mixed-breed dogs, not a special breed, which cuts against the folklore. **Verdict: this looks like popular history, not an established zooarchaeological finding** — same pattern as the sunflower case. The two Belize papers above are the only concrete leads left to actually test it.
- **ALT** Kiple's introduction (held) already gives the "few herds, tree fats" argument; it is now `fats-8000bc-1200bc::th6`.

### Mesoamerica, general build strategy (added 2026-10-25, updated same day)
Mesoamerica now has 7 real entries: `fats-8000bc-1200bc::te5` (no press technology), `fats-8000bc-1200bc::ec31` (disputed sunflower, CONTESTED), `fats-600-1000::ec13` (Maya oil palms — coyol, huiscoyol, cohune, the strongest entry found), `fats-1500-1750::ec19` and `::ec21` (two independent cacao-fat accounts, Oviedo and Landa), `fats-1500-1750::ec20` (Aztec guacamole), plus the avocado-domestication entries shared with the Andes. The coyol lead from the encyclopedia (below) resolved into the program's best Mesoamerica entry once the right book arrived.
- Checked two sources added 2026-10-25 and found nothing usable: *Turtle Island* (Sean Sherman et al., a modern Indigenous cookbook, not historical — it actually argues game meat is "naturally leaner," and has zero coverage of dogs as food) and the *Archaeology of Food* encyclopedia's Guilá Naquitz, Tehuacán Valley and Mesoamerican Archaic-Period Diet entries (all plant-only, no fat content, no fauna) — though the encyclopedia's Tehuacán entry is what first named coyol.
- **DONE 2026-10-25**: Christine D. White (ed.), *Reconstructing Ancient Maya Diet* (University of Utah Press, 1999) and Staller & Carrasco (eds.), *Pre-Columbian Foodways* (Springer, 2010) were both added and mined. White's book — specifically David Lentz's chapter 1, from his own Copán/Cerén fieldwork — gave the coyol/huiscoyol/cohune oil-palm entry, the program's clearest positive Mesoamerican fat finding. It also confirms, in Lentz's own words, that "most of [Maya] animal meat came from white-tailed deer, generally regarded as a lean meat source" despite the Maya having domesticated turkeys, Muscovy ducks and dogs — consistent with the dog/turkey-fat skepticism recorded above. The *Pre-Columbian Foodways* volume's epigraphic chapter (Kerry Hull) gave the Landa cacao-grease quote; its "Pastoral Niche" chapter, despite the title, turned out to be about maguey/textile production, not dog or turkey husbandry — no fat content there.
- The epigraphic-Maya-foodstuffs source on the earlier shortlist turned out to be a chapter *inside* the Pre-Columbian Foodways volume, not a separate paper — getting the book got both.
- **DONE 2026-10-25.** You found a related paper that settles the dog question with real evidence: van der Merwe, Tykot, Hammond and Oakberg, "Diet and Animal Husbandry of the Preclassic Maya at Cuello, Belize: Isotopic and Zooarchaeological Evidence," ch.2 in Ambrose and Katzenberg (eds.), *Biogeochemical Approaches to Paleodietary Analysis* (Kluwer Academic/Plenum, 2000) — not the exact Clutton-Brock & Hammond 1994 paper on the earlier list, but by an overlapping team (Hammond again) at the same site. Added as `fats-1200bc-300bc::ec16`: at Cuello, the earliest known Maya site (Preclassic, c.1200 BC–AD 300), dogs were slaughtered in their prime and show no isotopic sign of deliberate maize-fattening — they scavenged. The paper itself says fattening dogs and deer on maize is real, but a *later*, historic-period development, not yet present this early. This is now the program's actual answer to the dog-fattening question, replacing speculation with evidence.
- **Still open, lower priority given the above**: Clutton-Brock & Hammond 1994 itself, and White et al. 2001 on Colha, both paywalled. Piedras Negras (Latin American Antiquity) also still unobtained — likely the same isotope-can't-see-fat limitation as the PMC paper already read.

### Pacific Northwest Coast (new gap, found 2026-10-25)
- **DONE for now.** The program had nothing at all on eulachon ("candlefish") oil and the First Nations "grease trails" — one of the best-documented Indigenous fat economies in North America, missed simply because no one had looked. Added `fats-1960-today::ec16`, built from Megan Felicity Moody's 2008 UBC Master's thesis *Eulachon Past and Present* (hosted free on the Nuxalk Nation's own site, nuxalk.net/media/eulachon_moody.pdf): the Nuxalk Nation's own fermenting ("stink box") and cooking-box process on the Bella Coola River, from 2006 interviews with Nuxalk grease-makers, plus eulachon's fat content (15–25% of body weight, the highest of 26 forage-fish species studied) and the grease-trail trade network.
- **DONE 2026-10-25.** You found and added the paper itself (`j.jasrep.2018.11.033.pdf`): Patton, A.K., Martindale, A., Orchard, T.J., Vanier, S. and Coupland, G., "Finding eulachon: The use and cultural importance of *Thaleichthys pacificus* on the northern Northwest Coast of North America," *Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports* 23 (2019), pp.687–699. Read directly and added as `fats-8000bc-1200bc::ec30` — but note this paper is about the **Northern Tsimshian** fishery (Nass River, Prince Rupert Harbour), not the Nuxalk Nation's Bella Coola fishery in `fats-1960-today::ec16`; different people, different river, so it doesn't date that entry's own tradition. Radiocarbon dates push eulachon *fishing* back 2,500, perhaps 5,000, years — but the paper is honest that this is mostly evidence of eating dried/smoked fish, not oil-rendering specifically; the oft-cited "2000–3000 years old" figure for the rendering technology itself (MacDonald & Cove 1987) has, in this paper's own words, an unclear evidential basis.
